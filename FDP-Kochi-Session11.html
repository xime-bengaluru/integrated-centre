Your students' thinking is shaped by what you ask them to do. This session asks one uncomfortable question about what you are actually asking them to do.

The shift

In the last session you examined how you reason. Now examine what you are asking your students to do.

Most assessments test whether a student knows something. Very few test whether a student can think about what they know.

This session helps you find out which kind yours is.

The thinking gap test

One question that reveals
everything.

"Can a student score full marks on this assessment without interrogating a single assumption?"

Read your assessment again. Imagine a capable, well-prepared student who has memorised the content and can write fluently. Can they produce a distinction-level response without ever questioning a premise, testing a probability claim, checking for bias, or reasoning through genuine uncertainty?

If yes -- the assessment is measuring knowledge retrieval, not critical thinking. Even if we call it a case analysis.

Yes, they can score full marks without interrogating anything
No, genuine interrogation is required to score well

Apply all four tools to your assessment

Which ways of thinking does it
actually require?

Tool 01  ·  evaluate + test

Does this assessment require the student to reason honestly about probability or uncertainty? Or can they state conclusions as facts?

Tool 02  ·  seek + question

Does this assessment require the student to find and test the weakest link in a chain of reasoning -- including their own argument?

Tool 03  ·  generate + examine

Does this assessment require the student to identify and name assumptions -- their own or those embedded in the question itself?

Tool 04  ·  commit + account for it

Does this assessment require the student to make a defensible decision under genuine uncertainty and document the reasoning that led to it?

5

What the four-tool audit reveals

Most assessments fail
at least two of the four.

Look at your four answers. Which tools does your assessment genuinely require? Which ones does it only pretend to require -- where a student could technically satisfy the rubric without actually using that kind of thinking?

The gap between "required in theory" and "required in practice" is where the assessment loses its purpose. A case analysis that can be aced by summarising what happened does not develop the habit of asking what should have happened and why.

The tools my assessment actually requires

Be honest. The next step is to fix it.

6

The redesign

One change that makes
the requirement real.

Pick the one tool your assessment most conspicuously fails to require. Now make one specific change to the assessment that would force a student to use that tool in order to score well.

The change does not need to be large. It can be one sentence added to the question. One criterion added to the rubric. One requirement that students must name an assumption or assign a probability or defend a decision under uncertainty. Small changes make large differences to what thinking the room rewards.

The one change I will make

Specific and actionable. Something you could implement before the July term.

The takeaway from this session

An assessment that lets a student avoid thinking
is not an assessment. It is a performance.

The habit you are building in your classroom is the same habit you have been building in yourself this morning. The tools are the same. The discipline is the same. The only difference is whose thinking you are designing for.
